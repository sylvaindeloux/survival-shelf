# Fallen into cold water

> **In short**: the cold takes your breath away for the first minute: do not swim straight away. Float with your head out of the water until your breathing settles. Then call for help and get out as soon as you can, or hold on to anything that floats. Water below 15 °C (59 °F) already counts as cold water. Once out: get dry, get out of the wind, and warm up slowly.

## When to use it

- You fall from a bank, a jetty or a boat, or the ice gives way under you.
- You see someone fall into the water.
- Before a trip on the water or on a frozen lake.

## Before

1. **Wear a life jacket** on the water: when the cold numbs your arms and legs, it is what keeps you at the surface.
2. **Dress for the water temperature**, not the air temperature.
3. **Tell someone** where you are going and when you will be back.
4. **On ice**, find out how thick it is. The Canadian Red Cross asks for at least 15 cm (6 in) to walk or skate alone, 20 cm (8 in) for a group, 25 cm (10 in) for a snowmobile. If in doubt, stay off.

## During

### In the water

1. **Do not swim straight away.** Cold shock makes you gasp and your heart race: this is when people breathe in water. It passes in about a minute, sometimes two or three.
2. **Float**, relaxed, with your head out of the water. The air trapped in your clothes helps.
3. **When your breathing is back, call for help**, then get out of the water as soon as you can do so safely. Be quick: the cold then takes the strength out of your hands, arms and legs.
4. **If you cannot get out**, hold on to anything that floats and get as much of your body as possible out of the water.
5. **To keep your heat while you wait**: knees drawn up to your chest, legs together, arms against your sides, head out of the water. If there are several of you, huddle.

### The ice has given way

1. **Call for help.**
2. **Do not try to haul yourself straight out** at the edge of the hole: the ice is weak there.
3. **Get onto your stomach in the water**, put your arms on the ice in front of you without pushing down on it, kick your legs and slide your chest onto the ice.
4. **Do not stand up.** Crawl or roll away, arms and legs spread, all the way to the shore.

### You are a witness

1. **Do not go into the water or onto the ice.** Call the emergency services.
2. **Talk to the person** and tell them to float.
3. **From the shore**, lie down and reach out with a pole or a branch, or throw something that floats.

## After

1. **Get out of the wind, into a warm, dry place.**
2. **Take off wet clothes** and cover up with dry clothes and blankets, head included.
3. **Warm up slowly**, the trunk first: no hot bath, no hot water bottle, no rubbing.
4. **Call the emergency services** if the person will not stop shivering, slurs their words, is confused or drowsy: body temperature keeps dropping after leaving the water (see [Hypothermia and frostbite](../health-and-first-aid/hypothermia-and-frostbite.md)).
5. **Have a doctor examine** anyone who nearly drowned, even if they seem fine.

## Never do this

- Thrash about or swim as hard as you can during the first minute.
- Jump into the water to rescue someone.
- Stand up on the ice just after getting out.
- Warm someone up fast, in a hot bath or against a radiator.
- Give them alcohol.

"If in doubt, stay off" is common sense.
