# Cross a river

> **In short**: the safest crossing is the one you avoid: look for a bridge, wait for the water to drop, or turn back. Never cross water that is fast, murky, carrying branches or loud. If you must cross: at the widest point, shoes on, facing upstream with a pole, and ready to drop your pack.

## When to use it

- A stream or a river with no bridge cuts your route.
- A usual ford has swollen after rain or snowmelt.

## What you need

- A sturdy pole.
- Closed shoes.
- A waterproof bag for your phone, lighter, survival blanket and spare clothes.

## Steps

### Decide

1. **Before you leave**, find the unbridged crossings on the map, small side streams included, and check the weather for the whole catchment, not only for your route. Have a plan B: turn back, another route or waiting, with an extra day of food.
2. **Look at the water.** Do not cross if it is:
   - fast: faster than walking pace;
   - murky: brown, dirty;
   - messy: it carries branches or logs;
   - loud: it roars, or you can hear rocks rolling.
3. **Do not cross either** if the river is in flood, if there is no safe way in and out, if there are rapids or a waterfall downstream, or if nobody in the group has ever done it.
4. **Wait if you can**: the level can drop again fairly quickly. It changes with the time of day, the temperature and the rain that fell upstream: the US National Park Service advises crossing early in the day.

### Choose the spot

1. **Look for the widest part**, or where the river splits into several channels: it is usually the shallowest.
2. **Prefer a straight stretch.** Avoid the outside of bends, where the water is deep and swift.
3. **Work out where you will get out** on the far bank, and where you would end up if you fell.

### Cross

1. **Keep your shoes on.** Never barefoot.
2. **Carry on your body, kept dry**, your phone, a lighter and a survival blanket.
3. **Get your pack ready**: undo the waist strap and locate the buckles, so that you can shed it in one move if you fall.
4. **Alone**: face upstream, with the pole planted in front of you. Always keep two points of contact out of three and move only one foot or the pole at a time, sliding your feet along the bottom.
5. **In a group**: stand in a line along the current, the strongest person upstream, arms linked, and step sideways together.
6. **Turn back** if the water gets stronger or deeper than expected.

### If you are swept away

1. **Do not try to stand up** in the current.
2. **Lie on your back, feet pointing downstream**, toes out of the water: you push off rocks with your feet.
3. **Get rid of your pack** if it is in the way.
4. **Keep looking downstream** and make for the bank as soon as the current eases.

## Never do this

- Cross above rapids or a waterfall.
- Tie yourself to a rope: it can hold you under the water.
- Cross barefoot, or by hopping from rock to rock.
- Force the crossing because you are in a hurry or the goal is just across.

## Depending on where you are

- **Cold water**: see [Fallen into cold water](fallen-into-cold-water.md).
- **Flooded road or ford**: do not go in, on foot or by car (see [Flood](../disasters/flood.md)).
- **Downstream of a dam or after a thunderstorm**: the water can rise suddenly, do not linger in the riverbed.

The signs of an unsafe river come from New Zealand authorities, the method from the US National Park Service and the US Forest Service. These two services do not say the same thing about the pack: the Forest Service says to unbuckle the waist strap, while the Park Service points out that a loosened pack can throw you off balance. This guide keeps the unbuckled strap, so that you can free yourself. Making for the bank when the current eases is common sense.
