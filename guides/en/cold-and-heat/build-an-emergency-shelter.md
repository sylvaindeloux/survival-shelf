# Build an emergency shelter

> **In short**: first look for a shelter that already exists (car, building, rock overhang). Otherwise choose a flat spot, away from dead trees and riverbeds, insulate yourself from the ground with a thick layer of boughs or leaves, then put up a sloping roof that stops wind and rain. In freezing weather you last about 3 hours without shelter: start well before dark.

## When to use it

- You are stuck outside for the night: lost, injured, vehicle broken down far from anything.
- Bad weather is coming and you cannot reach a building.
- You must wait for rescue where you are.

## What you need

- If possible: a tarp, a poncho or an emergency blanket, some cord, a knife.
- On the spot: branches, dead leaves, dry grass, dry moss, snow.
- A light and gloves to work with.

## Steps

1. **Decide early.** If night is falling, if you are injured or exhausted, stop and spend the daylight you have left on the shelter.
2. **First look for an existing shelter** that needs little work: vehicle, building, hut, rock overhang, large fallen tree.
3. **Choose the site**: a flat spot, large enough to lie down. Avoid dead standing trees, dry riverbeds and gullies where water runs, and avalanche slopes.
4. **Turn the entrance sideways to the prevailing wind**, between 45° and 90°.
5. **Start with the ground.** Make a thick mattress of boughs, leaves or dry moss: it insulates you from the cold, damp ground.
6. **Put up the roof.**
   - With a tarp or a poncho: stretch it tight, with a steep slope of 40° to 60°, so that water runs off instead of soaking through.
   - Without a tarp: rest a long pole on a forked branch or a stump, lean branches against both sides to form a roof, then cover it with foliage, grass or bark, starting at the bottom and working up, like shingles.
7. **Close the sides exposed to the wind** with boughs, leaves or snow.
8. **Adapt the shelter to the weather**:
   - **Cold and snow**: you need an enclosed, insulated shelter. Snow is a good insulator: a trench dug in the snow and covered over, or a hole in a snowdrift, cuts the wind. Always keep an air vent. If you can no longer see your breath, the shelter is too warm: the snow will melt and wet you.
   - **Heat**: you need shade. Stretch two layers of material 30 to 45 cm (12 to 18 in) apart, and rest about 45 cm (18 in) above the hot ground or in a hollow of the same depth.
   - **Mild and wet weather**: protect yourself from wind and rain first.
9. **Make yourself visible**: brightly colored cloth or a shiny object, in an open spot near the shelter.
10. **If you make a fire**, light it well away from the shelter and from vegetation (see [Light a fire](../fire-and-power/light-a-fire.md)).

## Never do this

- Settle in a dry riverbed, under a dead tree or at the foot of an avalanche slope.
- Sleep directly on the ground.
- Seal a snow shelter completely, or burn anything in it without an air vent.
- Use a stove or a barbecue in a closed shelter or a tent: carbon monoxide.
- Leave a car stranded in snow to build a shelter: stay in the car.

## Without equipment

- No knife and no cord: pick branches that have already fallen and wedge them against each other, against a rock or a fallen trunk.
- Nothing for the roof: pile dead leaves or boughs in a very thick layer on the frame, and crawl under it.

## Depending on where you are

- **In a forest**: materials are plentiful. Look above you before settling: dead branches, leaning tree.
- **In the mountains and in snow**: get out of the wind behind a terrain feature, and dig rather than build.
- **In hot, dry country**: build early in the morning or in the evening, and rest in the shade during the hot hours.
- **In a town**: an entrance hall, a stairwell, a covered parking garage or a bus shelter already stop wind and rain.
