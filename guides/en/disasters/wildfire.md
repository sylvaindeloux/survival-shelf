# Wildfire

> **In short**: if you see a fire start, call the emergency services. If the fire comes close, follow the instructions of the authorities: leave at once when they say so. If you cannot leave, shelter in a solid building, close everything, block the vents and the bottom of the doors, and breathe through a damp cloth. A car is not a shelter: it can burn.

## When to use it

- Summer, drought, wind: the fire danger forecast is high or very high.
- You see smoke or flames in vegetation.
- Your home is in a forest or close to woods, moorland or scrub.

## What you need

- Cleared ground around the house.
- A garden hose that reaches the whole property.
- Cloths to wet, water, a battery radio.
- Your go-bag, ready.

## Before

1. **Clear the brush before summer**: it is the first protection of your home. Mow, cut the brush, prune low branches and remove anything that burns around the house.
2. **Clean the gutters**, and move the woodpile and the gas cylinders away from the house, into a closed shed.
3. **Do not start a fire yourself**: 9 fires out of 10 are caused by people.
   - Barbecue on a terrace, away from vegetation.
   - Cigarette ends in an ashtray. Never smoke in a forest.
   - Work that makes sparks away from dry grass, with a fire extinguisher at hand.
4. **Check the fire danger forecast** and the bans issued by the authorities: access to forests, barbecues, outdoor work.
5. **Plan several ways out**, and where to go.

## During

### You see a fire start

- Call the emergency services and give the location of the fire as precisely as you can.
- In a forest, leave the area at once.

### The fire is coming toward your home

1. **Follow the instructions** of the emergency services and the local authorities. If they say to evacuate, leave immediately.
2. **If you stay or cannot leave, shelter in the house** if it is a solid building with cleared ground around it.
3. **Bring the garden hose inside** before the fire arrives: you will use it afterwards to put out the last embers.
4. **Close doors, windows and shutters.**
5. **Block the vents and the bottom of the doors** with wet cloths: the smoke is toxic and embers get in everywhere.
6. **Breathe through a damp cloth.**

### You are in a car

- Do not take to the road to flee at the last moment, and do not take refuge in your car if a solid building is close.
- If the flames catch you in the car: stop in a clear area and do not get out.

### You are trapped

- Call the emergency services, give your position and turn on the lights to help the rescuers find you.

## After

1. **Do not go to the scene**, even if the fire is contained or out: you would hinder the firefighters, who may stay for several days.
2. **Put out the embers** around the house with the garden hose.
3. **Beware** of falling trees and rocks, hot ash and smoldering stumps. The ground can stay burning hot underneath.
4. **Protect yourself from the ash**: gloves, long sleeves, a mask. Children, pregnant women and people with lung or heart conditions must not breathe this dust.
5. **Check that the water is safe to drink** and throw away food exposed to heat or smoke.
6. **Before going back into the forest**, ask the authorities whether access is allowed.

## Never do this

- Go closer to watch or film.
- Flee by car through the smoke.
- Take refuge in a car when a house is close.
- Go home before the emergency services give the go-ahead.

## Depending on where you are

- **In an apartment building**: you are safer there. Close everything and evacuate only when ordered.
- **Camping, in a trailer or a tent**: these are not solid shelters. At the first alert, get to a solid building or leave the area as instructed.
- **Hiking**: when the danger is high, access to forests may be banned. Check before you leave.
- **In France**: clearing the brush is mandatory in the departments concerned, over 50 m (165 ft) around buildings that stand in a forest or less than 200 m (650 ft) from one. The exact rules are in the order issued by your prefecture. The "météo des forêts" gives the danger level for each department. The official advice is to stay sheltered in a cleared, solid house and to evacuate only when ordered.

The sources differ on when to leave. The American Red Cross and FEMA advise leaving as soon as you are told to, or as soon as you feel in danger. The French authorities advise staying sheltered in a solid house with cleared ground and evacuating only when ordered. This guide puts the instructions of your own authorities first. Two pieces of French advice seem to conflict and in fact complete each other: the Ministry for the Ecological Transition says not to take refuge in a car, which can burn; the civil protection service says that if flames catch you in a car, you stop in a clear area and stay inside.
