# Call for help without a phone signal

> **In short**: dial the emergency number anyway: an emergency call can go through the network of another operator. If nothing gets through, move to a high or open spot, or send someone to get help. Otherwise, stay where you are and make yourself seen and heard: whistle or flashlight in regular series, brightly colored cloth, large letters on the ground, smoke by day, fire by night.

## When to use it

- An accident, an injury or a breakdown in a place with no mobile coverage: mountains, forest, a remote road, at sea.
- The network has gone down after a disaster.
- You are trapped (rubble, vehicle, room) and nobody knows where you are.

## What you need

- A whistle: it carries farther than your voice and tires you less.
- A flashlight, a mirror or a shiny object.
- A brightly colored cloth or garment, a survival blanket.
- In remote areas: a personal locator beacon, registered in your name.

## Steps

### Keep trying to phone

1. **Dial the emergency number** (112 in Europe). Even if your own operator has no coverage at that spot, an emergency call can go through another operator's network: this is how it works in the United Kingdom and in Sweden, for example. If no operator covers the area, the call will not go through.
2. **Move** to a higher or more open spot, if you can do it safely, and try again.
3. **If there are several of you**, send someone to get help, with the exact place, the number of people and their condition written on a paper. Do not leave an injured person alone if you can avoid it.
4. **In a town, go straight** to the nearest police station, fire station or hospital, if you can do it safely.

### Get yourself spotted

1. **Stay put** once the alarm has been raised, or if someone knows your route: that is where they will look for you. Stay near your vehicle, which is far easier to see than a person.
2. **Make noise in regular series**: six whistle blasts in a row, repeated every minute, is the distress signal of British mountain rescue; in the United States, it is a series of three. Either way, a signal that repeats means "help". Keep going until someone reaches you.
3. **At night**, do the same with a flashlight.
4. **By day, reflect the sun** with a mirror or any shiny object, aiming at the plane, the helicopter or the people in the distance.
5. **Mark the ground** in an open place: the letters SOS, at least 3.5 m (12 ft) high, made of stones, branches or clothes that stand out against the ground, or traced in snow or sand. Straight lines and sharp corners show up better from above.
6. **Make smoke by day and fire by night**, if you can do it without starting a wildfire: green vegetation on a fire gives thick smoke.
7. **Hang a brightly colored cloth** up high, or wave it on the end of a stick.

### When a helicopter arrives

1. **Raise both arms in a V**: it means "yes, I need help". One arm up and the other down means "no".
2. **Put away anything that could blow away** and stay clear of a flat, open space about 45 m (150 ft) across.
3. **Do not go closer** until the crew tells you to, and always from the front or the side, never from the rear.

## Never do this

- Set off alone to look for help at night, in fog or when exhausted.
- Move after giving your position to the rescuers.
- Shout until you are exhausted: a whistle or knocking carries better.
- Light a signal fire in dry or windy weather, near vegetation.
- Set off a distress beacon when there is no real danger: it sends rescuers out for nothing.

## Without equipment

- **Trapped under rubble or in a room**: knock on a pipe or a wall in series of three, every one to two minutes, then listen.
- **In a car**: three blasts of the horn, three flashes of the headlights, a bright cloth on the antenna or at the window, the hood raised.
- **In the open**: wave both arms above your head.

## Depending on where you are

- **In France**: dial 112. You can also text 114, the emergency number for people who cannot speak or hear, with the exact place and what is happening: a written message sometimes gets through when voice does not.
- **In the mountains or in the forest**: before you leave, tell someone where you are going and when you will be back. Without a signal, that person is the one who will raise the alarm.
- **At sea**: VHF radio channel 16.
- **Once you get through**, say: what happened, the number of casualties and their condition, the exact place (altitude, route), the weather on site, your name and your number.

What happens to an emergency call when your own operator has no coverage is described by the UK Government and by Sweden's 112 operator, for the United Kingdom and for Sweden; this guide says nothing about other countries, or about calls made without a SIM card. Sources differ on the sound signal: six blasts a minute for British mountain rescue, series of three in the United States. This guide gives both. In France, 114 is meant for people who cannot speak or hear: using it when voice does not get through is a deduction, with no official source. The paper given to the person who goes for help is common sense.
