# Mountains and high altitude

> **In short**: in the mountains, the weather changes fast and help is far away: plan your outing, stay on the trails and give up at the slightest doubt. Above 2,500 m (8,200 ft), climb slowly: sleep no more than 400 m (1,300 ft) higher from one night to the next. Headache, nausea, great tiredness at altitude: stop climbing. If it does not pass or gets worse, go down.

## When to use it

- Before a hike, a trek or a trip above 2,000 m (6,600 ft).
- At altitude, when someone has a headache, feels sick or sleeps badly.

## What you need

- Suitable footwear and warm clothes, even in summer.
- Sunglasses, a cap, sunscreen.
- An up-to-date map, a compass, an altimeter or a GPS.
- A first-aid kit and survival blankets.
- A phone and, where there is no signal, a satellite phone or a radio.
- Water, food, a light.
- Depending on the terrain: helmet, crampons, ice axe, rope.

## Steps

### Prepare

1. **Ask mountain professionals** about the route and its condition, and hire a qualified guide as soon as the route is beyond your level.
2. **Check the weather forecast** and give up if it is bad.
3. **Tell someone** your route, your schedule, who is with you and how to reach you.
4. **Check your insurance**: not every policy covers search and evacuation costs in the mountains.
5. **Prepare the rest as for any outing** (see [Plan an outing](../outdoors/plan-an-outing.md)).

### On the ground

1. **Stay on marked trails.**
2. **Watch the sky**: a thunderstorm in the mountains means lightning (see [Caught in a thunderstorm](../outdoors/caught-in-a-thunderstorm.md)).
3. **Beware of a sudden thaw**: it raises the risk of avalanches and rockfall.
4. **Do not linger in the bed of a mountain stream or downstream of a dam**: the water can rise suddenly there.
5. **Drink regularly** and protect yourself from the sun.
6. **Be ready to give up** and turn back.

### Climb without getting sick

1. **Altitude sickness can start from 2,000 m (6,600 ft)** and can affect anyone, however fit.
2. **From 2,500 m (8,200 ft), do not sleep more than 400 m (1,300 ft) higher than the night before.**
3. **Spend a few days below 2,500 m (8,200 ft)** before going higher.
4. **From 3,000 m (9,800 ft), take a rest day at the same altitude** every 3 to 4 days.
5. **Do not go from below 1,200 m (3,900 ft) to above 3,500 m (11,500 ft) in one day.** If you fly straight to high altitude, rest for 1 day.
6. **Drink enough water, avoid alcohol** and hard effort for the first two days.

### If altitude sickness sets in

1. **Recognize it.** The signs usually come 6 to 10 hours after arrival: headache, loss of appetite, feeling or being sick, tiredness, dizziness, poor sleep.
2. **Tell** the people with you.
3. **Stop climbing** and rest at the same altitude until you feel better. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen eases the headache.
4. **Go down by 300 to 1,000 m (1,000 to 3,300 ft)** if you are no better after 1 day, or as soon as the signs get worse.
5. **Go down at once and call for help** if you see any of these signs:
   - confusion, hallucinations;
   - loss of balance, poor coordination;
   - shortness of breath even at rest;
   - a cough with frothy or blood-stained spit;
   - blue or gray lips or skin;
   - great drowsiness, a person who is hard to wake.
6. **Never let the person go down alone.**

## Never do this

- Keep climbing with signs of altitude sickness.
- Believe that being fit protects you.
- Leave someone who is unwell on their own.
- Drink alcohol at altitude.
- Leave the trail to save time.

## Depending on where you are

- **In France**: dial 112.
- **At very high altitude**: for a serious case, the French foreign ministry asks you to go down as low as possible, and at least below 4,000 m (13,100 ft).
- **In winter and off the marked runs**: see [Avalanche](../disasters/avalanche.md) and [Snow and extreme cold](snow-and-extreme-cold.md).

Sources differ on the rate of ascent: the French foreign ministry sets 400 m (1,300 ft) between two nights from 2,500 m (8,200 ft), while the British NHS and the Quebec public health institute set 500 m (1,600 ft) a day above 3,000 m (9,800 ft). This guide keeps the more cautious benchmark. The NHS usually places altitude sickness above 2,500 m (8,200 ft), the French foreign ministry from 2,000 m (6,600 ft). Never letting the person go down alone is common sense.
