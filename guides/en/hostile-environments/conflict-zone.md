# Conflict zone

> **In short**: the safest thing is not to be there: leave while the roads are open. On the spot, travel by day, on paved and busy roads, and never where local people do not go. Do not touch any object on the ground and do not enter abandoned buildings. Facing armed men, stop and do what they say.

## When to use it

- You live or travel in a region where there is fighting, or which is coming out of a conflict.
- You have to cross an area where mines or unexploded munitions remain.

To shelter from gunfire and shelling at home, see [Armed conflict and shelling](../disasters/armed-conflict-and-shelling.md).

## What you need

- Enough for 3 days: water, food that keeps, a first-aid kit, a light, a radio, survival blankets.
- Everyone's documents, cash in small bills.
- A phone, its charger and a power bank.
- The numbers of your embassy or consulate, the local police and the local rescue services.
- A vehicle whose tank is kept full.

## Steps

### Before, and while things are calm

1. **Read your country's travel advice** before you leave, and give up the trip if the area is advised against.
2. **Register with your foreign ministry's alert service** if it has one: for French nationals, the free "Fil d'Ariane" service sends security alerts.
3. **Pack one bag per person** and check it every six months.
4. **Ask local people** which places are dangerous, and always tell someone where you are going.
5. **Leave early.** When an evacuation is organized, it is the last resort: follow to the letter the instructions of those in charge of it.

### Getting around

1. **Keep to main roads that are paved and busy.** Do not take a road you do not know unless others have just used it.
2. **Drive by day**, seat belt on, and do not be the first on the road in the morning.
3. **Never leave the road surface**: not to get around an obstacle, not to let someone pass, not for a break. If the road is blocked, turn back.
4. **Where armed people control the place**, stop and follow their instructions.
5. **Empty checkpoint with mines on the road**: do not move them, assume you are being watched and, if nobody comes, turn back.
6. **Warning shots** mean you are not welcome: do not press on.

### Mines and explosive objects

1. **Learn the signs.**
   - Official: red sign, skull and crossbones, "DANGER MINES", tape.
   - Improvised: a piece of cloth or a plastic bag tied to a fence or a tree, a small pile of stones, a can on a post, sticks, tape.
   - Clues: craters, destroyed or abandoned vehicles, overgrown fields, animal carcasses, disturbed soil, places local people avoid, an unusually empty road.
2. **Never go into a marked area.** Many mined areas are not marked.
3. **Do not approach or touch any mine, any munition, any suspicious object**, for any reason. No "souvenirs".
4. **Avoid** old fighting positions, deserted houses, country tracks, gardens and overgrown land, destroyed military equipment.
5. **Do not use a radio or a phone within 100 m (330 ft)** of a mine or a munition you have spotted: move away first.
6. **You see a suspicious object**: stop, touch nothing, go back the way you came, then warn the others and the authorities.

### You think you are in a mined area

1. **On foot**:
   - stop at once and do not move your feet;
   - look at the ground around you: mines, wires, signs;
   - warn the others, call the emergency services, keep everyone away;
   - move only if you can see how to reach the last hard ground you knew was safe, a paved road or concrete; otherwise, wait for help.
2. **In a car**:
   - stop, do not reverse, do not turn the wheel;
   - stay inside, sound the horn to attract attention and wait for qualified help;
   - if you must get out, for example because of a fire, get out without stepping on the ground beside the vehicle and go back along the wheel tracks, one person at a time.
3. **Someone is injured in the area**: do not run to them. Call the emergency services and tell them not to move.

### Under fire

1. **Shelling, outdoors**: throw yourself into a ditch or behind solid cover.
2. **In a car, if the shells are landing very close**, get out and lie flat in a ditch or behind solid cover, not under the vehicle.
3. **Small-arms fire**: stay indoors, away from windows, with at least two walls between you and the shooting.
4. **After an explosion, do not rush to the scene**: there may be more shooting or a second device.
5. **Wait 5 to 10 minutes after the firing stops** before going out, and watch what local people do.

## Never do this

- Pick up or move a munition or a piece of military debris.
- Go into an abandoned house or search destroyed equipment.
- Drive on the shoulder, cut across fields or follow an unknown track.
- Drive at night.
- Go where local people do not go.
- Run to an injured person in an area that may be mined.

## Depending on where you are

- **French nationals abroad**: only the ambassador and the security officer decide on a regrouping or an evacuation. Follow the instructions of the embassy and of your local warden, not rumors. On an evacuation flight, luggage is often limited to 10 kg (22 lb).
- **If your children are at school**: do not rush to fetch them, unless you are asked to.
- **At home**: see [Civil war](../disasters/civil-war.md).

This guide relies on an International Committee of the Red Cross guide written in 2005 for humanitarian volunteers, on the United Nations Mine Action Service handbook and, for what to do once inside a mined area, on the Ukrainian site Dovidka.info, whose page does not name its publisher. The pages of the ICRC guide on checkpoints could not be read: this guide keeps to the general rule of following the instructions of those who control the place. Sources differ on phones: the ICRC says not to use one within 100 m (330 ft) of a mine, while the Ukrainian page says to call the emergency services when you are stuck in a mined area. This guide keeps both: move away first when you can, call when you can no longer move. Leaving early and giving up a trip that is advised against are common sense.
